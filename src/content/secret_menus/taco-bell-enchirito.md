While Taco Bell periodically revives classic items through limited-time in-app promotions, few retired menu icons generate as many daily counter requests as the **Enchirito**. First introduced in the late 1960s and officially removed from permanent menu boards in 2013, this hybrid enchilada-burrito remains a legend among fast-food enthusiasts.

To the customer, an Enchirito is simply a bean and beef burrito served in a plastic bowl, smothered in red sauce, and topped with melted cheese. To a quick-service restaurant (QSR) general manager monitoring drive-thru timer performance, an ad-hoc Enchirito request during peak dinner rush is an operational headache. Here is what actually happens on the Taco Bell makeline when an Enchirito is ordered, why kitchen crews dread making it, and how to order it without destroying line throughput.

## 1. Anatomy of the Smothered Build

![The Taco Bell Enchirito Why Kitchens Dread the Smothered Secret Menu Hack](../../assets/images/general/generic-fryer.webp)



The standard Taco Bell kitchen assembly line is engineered for speed, relying on dry paper wrapping and rapid handoffs. A standard Bean Burrito requires roughly 12 seconds of active assembly time. Converting that burrito into an Enchirito changes the entire mechanical workflow:

### The Steamer and Melter Sequence
Instead of wrapping the rolled tortilla in standard thermal paper, the line worker must divert from the standard dry-packaging protocol:

1. **Base Assembly:** The worker portions 1.5 ounces of [Rehydrated Refried Beans](/articles/taco-bell-rehydrate-beans), 1.5 ounces of seasoned beef, and a pinch of diced onions onto a 10-inch flour tortilla, rolling it into a standard cylinder.
2. **Container Routing:** Instead of moving to the wrap station, the rolled burrito is placed seam-side down into a black plastic microwave-safe bowl (the same tray used for Mexican Pizzas and Power Bowls).
3. **The Smothering Stage:** The worker grabs the red sauce ladle from the heated steam table and pours two full 1-ounce scoops directly over the burrito, completely coating the flour tortilla.
4. **Cheese Capping:** A generous 2-ounce pinch of 3-cheese blend (mozzarella, cheddar, and Monterey Jack) is scattered across the red sauce.
5. **Thermal Melting:** The bowl is fed into the commercial [Conveyor Steamer/Melter](/articles/taco-bell-steamer-melter). The 350°F steam chamber melts the cheese emulsion over the red sauce in approximately 18 seconds before the tray exits onto the expediting counter.

<div class="callout callout-tip">**ProTip:** Ask for the three-cheese blend to be added before the red sauce. The sauce insulates the cheese in the steamer, preventing it from turning into a rubbery puck.</div>



Because the Enchirito deviates from standard thermal paper packaging, it also disrupts the expediter's rhythm. When a drive-thru order consists of four crunchy tacos and one Enchirito, the expeditor has to find a flat-bottomed bag large enough to hold the black plastic bowl securely, while ensuring the tacos do not crush it. This specific packaging tetris slows down the final bagging process by at least ten seconds per order. If the lid is not snapped on perfectly tight, that scalding red sauce will leak out during the handoff, resulting in a ruined bag, a burned customer, and an immediate voided ticket.

## 2. Why The Kitchen Dreads It (Annoyance Score: 8/10)

Taco Bell kitchens operate under strict labor efficiency goals, with drive-thru window times (DTO/DTR) targeted at under 50 seconds per vehicle during lunch and dinner rushes. When an off-menu Enchirito is keyed into the POS register, it causes three operational disruptions:

* **Steamer Queue Jamming:** The conveyor steamer is the primary bottleneck in a Taco Bell kitchen, shared by Mexican Pizzas, Cheesy Gordita Crunches, and Quesadillas. A smothered Enchirito bowl takes up significant width on the steamer belt. During a rush, tying up the steamer with an off-menu item delays every other hot item in the ticket queue.
* **Sauce Splatter and Cleanliness:** Ladling hot red sauce into a shallow plastic tray on a fast-moving steel makeline inevitably leads to splashing. Workers frequently get red sauce on the outer rim of the tray or onto the assembly table. To maintain corporate food safety and presentation standards, the [Linebacker or Expeditor](/articles/taco-bell-linebacker-role) must stop to carefully wipe the tray rim clean before sealing the clear plastic lid.
* **Ticket Readability Chaos:** Because "Enchirito" does not exist as a standard POS button on modern registers, cashiers must ring up a complex string of modifiers: `BEAN BURRITO -> ADD BEEF -> ADD ONIONS -> ADD RED SAUCE -> ADD 3-CHEESE -> SPECIAL INSTRUCTION: IN BOWL / STEAMED`. On the Kitchen Display System (KDS) monitor, this lengthy text block takes up half the screen, slowing down the line workers' reading comprehension and increasing the chance of an assembly error.

<div class="callout callout-tip">
  <strong>Manager's Tip: How to Order Without Hurting Window Times</strong>
  If you want an Enchirito without earning the wrath of the kitchen crew, avoid ordering it at the drive-thru speaker during peak rush hours (12:00 PM to 1:30 PM). 
  <br><br>
  Instead, place your order inside the dining room or via the mobile kiosk. Order a **"Bean Burrito with Beef and Onions,"** and order sides of **"Red Sauce"** and **"3-Cheese Blend"** in separate plastic ramekins. When your order arrives, dump your burrito into the paper bag or onto a plate, pour the side of red sauce and cheese over it yourself, and enjoy. You get the exact same flavor profile without forcing the line crew to disrupt their steamer queue.
</div>
